The Elements of Professionalism · Part Seven

What you can expect from me

Six letters so far, and every one of them has asked something of you. This one is the other direction. These are the commitments I make to you, and this week I had to act on one of them.

This morning I terminated a management client. Two properties, both revenue we were collecting, both gone as of the first of November.

I am not going to name them or go through the history. Some of you lived it, and the rest do not need the details to understand the point.

Here is what matters. The relationship had reached the stage where our team was being told how to do its job, where professional determinations were treated as obstacles to be worn down, and where no answer was ever accepted as final. Every exchange took longer, cost more, and ended further from resolution than it started.

I could have kept that account. I chose not to, and I want you to know why.

What it would have cost to keep it

Keeping it would have been easy. I could have handed it down the chain, kept the management fee, and let whoever caught it absorb the weekly grind. That is what a great many companies do, and it is the obvious business decision — the revenue is real and the cost of keeping it does not appear anywhere you can point to.

The cost of that decision does not appear anywhere on a profit and loss statement, which is exactly why it gets made so often. But it is real, and it is expensive.

What it actually costs

  • It costs the person holding it. Someone opens their laptop on Monday morning and feels their stomach drop before they have read a word. That is not a minor thing, and it does not stay confined to one account.
  • It costs our standards. If pressure works often enough, people stop applying the standard and start managing the pressure. That is how a well-run company quietly becomes a reactive one.
  • It costs our good people. Nobody resigns over one difficult client. They resign after a year of being told that absorbing it is part of the job.
  • It costs our other owners. Hours spent in a relationship that cannot be satisfied are hours not spent on owners who value what we do.

A management fee is not worth the slow erosion of a team. I would rather explain a smaller number at the end of the year than watch good people learn that their judgment is for sale.

What I am committing to

Six things. I would rather write them down than have you guess.

  1. I will not sell your professional judgment for a management fee. When you reach a determination through our process and apply it honestly, that determination stands. An owner is entitled to disagree with it. They are not entitled to overturn it by persistence.
  2. When you are right, I will back you — out loud, to the client. Not privately while something softer goes out under my name. If I disagree with how you handled something, you will hear it from me directly and first.
  3. I will not ask you to absorb abuse as part of the job. Difficult is part of the job. Demanding is part of the job. Being worn down, spoken to poorly, or treated as the problem by default is not, and I will not quietly ask you to tolerate it because the revenue is convenient.
  4. I will make the hard call so you do not have to. Ending a client relationship is my decision and my name on the letter. Your job is to tell me what is happening. The uncomfortable part is mine.
  5. I will tell you why, not just what. You should never have to guess at the reasoning behind a decision that affects your work. If I cannot explain it, it probably was not a good decision.
  6. I will keep choosing the team over the invoice. Not as a slogan — as an actual decision, repeatedly, including when it costs money. This week it cost money.

An important line, so this is not misread

I want to be careful here, because a letter like this can be taken further than I intend.

This is not about owners who are frustrated. A frustrated owner usually has a reason, and our job is to find it and address it. Someone who is upset about a vacancy, a repair bill or a decision they did not expect is not a problem client — that is an ordinary Tuesday, and handling it well is the work.

It is not about owners who disagree with us, either. Owners are entitled to push back, to ask hard questions, and to expect us to justify our positions. That pressure makes us better and we should welcome it.

What is different

It is a pattern rather than an incident. No answer is ever accepted. Every explanation produces a new demand. The team is cast as the problem by default. The relationship stops being a disagreement about a decision and becomes a question about whether our judgment counts at all.

That is a different thing, and it is rare. In twenty-five years I have ended a handful of relationships this way. It should stay rare.

What I ask in return

Three things, and they are small.

  • Tell me early. I cannot protect you from something I find out about in month four. The sooner I know a relationship is going sideways, the more options we both have.
  • Work the process. I can back a determination reached properly, documented honestly, and delivered professionally. I cannot back a shortcut, and you would not want me to.
  • Stay professional even when they are not. Every exchange in a difficult relationship may end up read by someone else. Make sure ours is the side that reads well. This week, it did — and that made the decision straightforward rather than complicated.

You do the work properly. I will handle what comes back. That is the arrangement, and it is not conditional on what it costs.

Thank you, all of you, for the way you show up.

Pamela
